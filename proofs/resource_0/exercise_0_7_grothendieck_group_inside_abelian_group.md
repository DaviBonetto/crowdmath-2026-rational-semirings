## Restatement

Let $A$ be an abelian group and let $M$ be a submonoid of $A$. We want to find a subgroup $G$ of $A$ that sits between $M$ and $A$ (i.e., $M \subseteq G \subseteq A$) and is isomorphic to the Grothendieck group $\mathcal{G}(M)$.

In other words, $M$ already lives inside an ambient abelian group $A$, and we want to show that the "best group containing $M$" — its Grothendieck group — can be realized as an actual subgroup of $A$, not just abstractly.

---

## Definitions Used

- **Submonoid.** A subset $M \subseteq A$ that is closed under the binary operation of $A$ and contains the identity element $0_A$.
- **Cancellative monoid.** A monoid $M$ in which the cancellation law holds: $m + a = m + b \implies a = b$ for all $m, a, b \in M$.
- **Grothendieck group** $\mathcal{G}(M)$. Given a commutative cancellative monoid $(M, +)$, the Grothendieck group is the set of equivalence classes $(M \times M)/{\sim}$, where
  $$
  (a, b) \sim (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c.
  $$
  We write the class of $(a,b)$ as $[a, b]$ (think of it as the formal difference $a - b$). The group operation is $[a,b] + [c,d] = [a+c,\, b+d]$, the identity is $[0_M, 0_M]$, and the inverse of $[a,b]$ is $[b,a]$. The canonical embedding $\iota \colon M \to \mathcal{G}(M)$ is $\iota(m) = [m, 0_M]$.
- **Universal property of $\mathcal{G}(M)$.** For every abelian group $H$ and every monoid homomorphism $f \colon M \to H$, there exists a unique group homomorphism $F \colon \mathcal{G}(M) \to H$ such that $F \circ \iota = f$.

---

## Proof

**Step 1 — $M$ is cancellative.**

Since $M$ is a submonoid of the abelian group $A$, the cancellation law of $A$ restricts to $M$: if $m, a, b \in M$ and $m + a = m + b$ in $M$, then the same equality holds in $A$, giving $a = b$. Hence $M$ is a commutative cancellative monoid, so $\mathcal{G}(M)$ is well defined.

**Step 2 — Construct the candidate subgroup $G$.**

Define

$$
G := \{a - b \in A : a, b \in M\}.
$$

We verify that $G$ is a subgroup of $A$:

- _Identity._ Since $M$ is a submonoid of $A$, it contains $0_A$. Thus $0_A = 0_A - 0_A \in G$.
- _Closure._ If $a - b,\, c - d \in G$ (with $a,b,c,d \in M$), then $(a-b)+(c-d) = (a+c)-(b+d) \in G$, since $a+c, b+d \in M$ (as $M$ is closed under $+$).
- _Inverses._ If $a - b \in G$, then $-(a-b) = b - a \in G$.

So $G \leq A$. Moreover:

- $M \subseteq G$: for any $m \in M$, write $m = m - 0_A \in G$.
- $G \subseteq A$: by construction.

Hence $M \subseteq G \subseteq A$.

**Step 3 — Define the isomorphism $F \colon \mathcal{G}(M) \to G$.**

Consider the inclusion map $\varphi \colon M \hookrightarrow A$, which is a monoid homomorphism (it is an injective map that preserves the operation and the identity). By the **universal property** of $\mathcal{G}(M)$, there exists a unique group homomorphism

$$
F \colon \mathcal{G}(M) \longrightarrow A
$$

such that $F \circ \iota = \varphi$, i.e., $F([m, 0_M]) = m$ for all $m \in M$.

Explicitly, $F$ is given by

$$
F([a, b]) = a - b \quad (a, b \in M).
$$

**Well-definedness.** If $[a, b] = [c, d]$ in $\mathcal{G}(M)$, then $a + d = b + c$ in $M$, hence also in $A$, so $a - b = c - d$ in $A$.

**Group homomorphism.** For $[a,b], [c,d] \in \mathcal{G}(M)$:

$$
F([a,b]+[c,d]) = F([a+c,\, b+d]) = (a+c)-(b+d) = (a-b)+(c-d) = F([a,b])+F([c,d]).
$$

**Step 4 — The image of $F$ is exactly $G$.**

$$
\operatorname{Im}(F) = \{F([a,b]) : a,b \in M\} = \{a - b : a,b \in M\} = G.
$$

So we may restrict the codomain and regard $F$ as a surjective group homomorphism $F \colon \mathcal{G}(M) \twoheadrightarrow G$.

**Step 5 — $F$ is injective.**

Suppose $F([a,b]) = 0_A$. Then $a - b = 0_A$, so $a = b$ in $A$. Since $M \subseteq A$, this means $a = b$ as elements of $M$. Therefore

$$(a, b) \sim (0_M, 0_M),$$

because $a + 0_M = b + 0_M$. Hence $[a,b] = [0_M, 0_M]$, so the kernel of $F$ is trivial. Thus $F$ is injective.

**Conclusion.**

The map $F \colon \mathcal{G}(M) \to G$ is a bijective group homomorphism, hence a group isomorphism. Combined with the chain $M \subseteq G \subseteq A$ established in Step 2, we conclude that $G$ is a subgroup of $A$ with $M \subseteq G \subseteq A$ and $G \cong \mathcal{G}(M)$. $\blacksquare$

## Notes

- The whole proof hinges on $M$ being cancellative, otherwise $\mathcal{G}(M)$ isn't even well defined. But since $M$ sits inside a group, cancellation comes for free, so we never need to check it separately.

- Worth noting: $G = \{a - b : a,b \in M\}$ is literally the subgroup of $A$ generated by $M$, i.e., $G = \langle M \rangle_A$. Any subgroup containing $M$ must contain all such differences, and $G$ is already a subgroup, so it's the smallest one.

- The point here is that when $M$ already lives inside an abelian group, its Grothendieck group isn't some abstract quotient — it can be identified with the concrete subgroup $\langle M \rangle_A$. The formal difference $[a,b]$ just becomes the actual difference $a - b$ in $A$.

- There's a slicker way to see the same thing via the universal property: the inclusion $M \hookrightarrow A$ extends to a unique group homomorphism $F \colon \mathcal{G}(M) \to A$, and its image is $G$. Injectivity of $F$ follows because the inclusion is injective and $M$ is cancellative.

- The map $F$ is canonical — it's the _unique_ extension of the inclusion $M \hookrightarrow A$ to a group homomorphism out of $\mathcal{G}(M)$. So the isomorphism $\mathcal{G}(M) \cong G$ isn't a choice; it's forced by the universal property.
