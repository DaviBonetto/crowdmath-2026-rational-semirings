## Restatement

Let $M$ be a monoid and let $S \subseteq M$ be any subset. We want to show that the submonoid generated by $S$, denoted $\langle S \rangle$, equals the set of all finite products of elements of $S$, that is,

$$
\langle S \rangle = \left\{ \prod_{i=1}^{n} s_i : n \in \mathbb{N}_0 \text{ and } s_1, \ldots, s_n \in S \right\}.
$$

## Definitions Used

- **Monoid:** A set $M$ equipped with an associative binary operation and an identity element $1_M$.

- **Submonoid:** A subset $N \subseteq M$ that contains $1_M$ and is closed under the operation of $M$.

- **Submonoid generated by $S$:** The smallest submonoid of $M$ containing $S$, denoted $\langle S \rangle$. More precisely,

  $$
  \langle S \rangle = \bigcap \{ N \subseteq M : N \text{ is a submonoid of } M \text{ and } S \subseteq N \}.
  $$

- **Empty product:** By convention, the empty product (when $n = 0$) equals the identity element $1_M$.

## Proof

Define the set of all finite products of elements of $S$ by

$$
P := \left\{ \prod_{i=1}^{n} s_i : n \in \mathbb{N}_0,\ s_1, \ldots, s_n \in S \right\}.
$$

We will show that $\langle S \rangle = P$ by proving both inclusions.

---

**Step 1: $P$ is a submonoid of $M$ containing $S$.**

We verify the three required properties:

- _Identity:_ Taking $n = 0$, the empty product equals $1_M$ by convention. Hence $1_M \in P$.

- _Closure:_ Let $x, y \in P$. Then there exist $n, m \in \mathbb{N}_0$ and elements $s_1, \ldots, s_n, t_1, \ldots, t_m \in S$ such that $x = \prod_{i=1}^n s_i$ and $y = \prod_{j=1}^m t_j$. By associativity in $M$,

  $$
  xy = s_1 \cdots s_n \cdot t_1 \cdots t_m = \prod_{k=1}^{n+m} u_k,
  $$

  where $u_k = s_k$ for $k \leq n$ and $u_k = t_{k-n}$ for $k > n$. In particular, each $u_k \in S$ and the product has $n + m \in \mathbb{N}_0$ factors, so $xy \in P$.

- _$S \subseteq P$:_ For any $s \in S$, take $n = 1$ and $s_1 = s$, giving $s = \prod_{i=1}^1 s_i \in P$.

Hence $P$ is a submonoid of $M$ with $S \subseteq P$.

---

**Step 2: $\langle S \rangle \subseteq P$.**

Since $\langle S \rangle$ is the **smallest** submonoid of $M$ containing $S$, and $P$ is a submonoid of $M$ with $S \subseteq P$ (by Step 1), it follows directly from the definition of $\langle S \rangle$ that

$$
\langle S \rangle \subseteq P.
$$

---

**Step 3: $P \subseteq \langle S \rangle$.**

We prove by induction on $n \in \mathbb{N}_0$ that every product $\prod_{i=1}^n s_i$ with $s_i \in S$ belongs to $\langle S \rangle$.

- _Base case ($n = 0$):_ The empty product is $1_M$, which belongs to $\langle S \rangle$ since $\langle S \rangle$ is a monoid.

- _Inductive step:_ Suppose every product of $n$ elements of $S$ belongs to $\langle S \rangle$. Given $s_1, \ldots, s_{n+1} \in S$, write
  $$
  \prod_{i=1}^{n+1} s_i = \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} s_i\right) \cdot s_{n+1}.
  $$
  By the induction hypothesis, $\prod_{i=1}^n s_i \in \langle S \rangle$. Moreover, $s_{n+1} \in S \subseteq \langle S \rangle$. Since $\langle S \rangle$ is closed under the operation of $M$, we conclude that $\prod_{i=1}^{n+1} s_i \in \langle S \rangle$.

By induction, every element of $P$ belongs to $\langle S \rangle$, i.e., $P \subseteq \langle S \rangle$.

---

**Conclusion.**

Combining Steps 2 and 3, we obtain

$$
\langle S \rangle = P = \left\{ \prod_{i=1}^{n} s_i : n \in \mathbb{N}_0 \text{ and } s_1, \ldots, s_n \in S \right\}. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

## Notes

- The choice of $\mathbb{N}_0$ (rather than $\mathbb{N}$) as the domain for $n$ is essential to capture the identity element via the empty product; without it, $P$ might fail to be a submonoid.

- The argument in Step 2 relies solely on the universal property of $\langle S \rangle$ as the smallest submonoid containing $S$, requiring no additional hypotheses on $M$ such as commutativity or cancellativity.

- An alternative proof of $P \subseteq \langle S \rangle$ (without explicit induction) observes directly that any submonoid containing $S$ must be closed under arbitrary finite products of elements of $S$. Since $\langle S \rangle$ contains $S$, it contains all such products, giving $P \subseteq \langle S \rangle$ without formalizing the induction on $n$.

- The result generalizes naturally to non-commutative monoids, as the order of the factors in each product is preserved throughout the proof.
