## Restatement

Given the additive monoid $M_q = (\mathbb{N}_0[q], +)$ for $q \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, we want to:
1. Determine all values of $q$ for which $M_q$ is an atomic monoid.
2. For each of these values, explicitly identify the set of atoms of $M_q$.

## Definitions Used

- **Atom / irreducible element:** A non-zero element in an additive commutative monoid that cannot be written as a sum of two non-zero elements.
- **Atomic Monoid:** A monoid where every non-zero element can be expressed as a finite sum of atoms.
- **Reduced form of $q$:** We write $q = a/b$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

## Proof

Let $q = a/b$ in reduced form ($a,b \in \mathbb{N}, \gcd(a,b)=1$). We analyze the atomicity of $M_q = \mathbb{N}_0[q]$ in three disjoint cases based on the values of $a$ and $b$.

### Case 1: $b = 1$ (i.e., $q \in \mathbb{N}$)

If $q$ is a positive integer, then any power $q^i$ is also a positive integer. Thus, all elements of $\mathbb{N}_0[q]$ are integers, and since $q^0 = 1 \in M_q$, we have $M_q = \mathbb{N}_0$.
The monoid $(\mathbb{N}_0, +)$ is generated by $1$, and its only atom is $1$. Since every non-zero element is a finite sum of $1$s, $M_q$ is atomic.
- **Is it atomic?** Yes.
- **Set of atoms:** $\{1\}$.

### Case 2: $a = 1$ and $b \ge 2$, so $q = 1/b$

We show that $M_q$ has no atoms.

Let $x \in M_q \setminus \{0\}$. Since $M_q$ is closed under multiplication by $q$, we have $qx \in M_q$. Also $qx \neq 0$. Since $q = 1/b$, we get

$$x = b(qx).$$

In additive notation,

$$x = \underbrace{qx + \cdots + qx}_{b\text{ times}}.$$

Because $b \ge 2$, this expresses $x$ as a sum of nonzero elements of $M_q$. Therefore no nonzero element of $M_q$ is an atom. Hence $M_q$ is not atomic.
- **Is it atomic?** No.

### Case 3: $a \ge 2$ and $b \ge 2$

We claim that in this case, $M_q$ is atomic and its set of atoms is exactly $\mathcal{A} = \{q^n : n \in \mathbb{N}_0\}$.

To prove this, we must show that every $q^n$ is an atom. Suppose for contradiction that $q^n$ can be decomposed into two non-zero elements:
$$ q^n = x + y \quad \text{for some } x, y \in M_q \setminus \{0\}. $$

We split this into two subcases depending on whether $q > 1$ or $q < 1$:

#### Subcase 3a: $q > 1$ (which means $a > b \ge 2$)

Since $q > 1$, the sequence of powers $q^i$ is strictly increasing. Any non-zero term constructed using powers $q^i$ for $i \ge n$ will be strictly greater than or equal to $q^n$. If either $x$ or $y$ uses a power $\ge n$, their sum $x+y$ would strictly exceed $q^n$ (since the other element is non-zero).
Therefore, $x$ and $y$ must be linear combinations of strictly smaller powers: $1, q, \ldots, q^{n-1}$.
This implies $q^n \in \langle 1, q, \ldots, q^{n-1} \rangle$, so we can write:
$$ q^n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} c_i q^i \quad (c_i \in \mathbb{N}_0) $$
Substituting $q = a/b$:
$$ \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} c_i \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^i $$
Multiplying both sides by $b^n$ to clear denominators:
$$ a^n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} c_i a^i b^{n-i} $$
Notice that every term on the right-hand side contains at least one factor of $b$ (since $i \le n-1 \implies n-i \ge 1$). Thus, $b$ must divide $a^n$.
However, since $\gcd(a,b) = 1$, $b$ cannot divide $a^n$ unless $b = 1$. This directly contradicts our assumption that $b \ge 2$.
Hence, $q^n$ cannot be decomposed, making it an atom.

#### Subcase 3b: $0 < q < 1$ (which means $b > a \ge 2$)

Since $0 < q < 1$, the sequence of powers $q^i$ is strictly decreasing. Any non-zero term constructed using powers $q^i$ for $i \le n$ will be strictly greater than or equal to $q^n$. If either $x$ or $y$ uses a power $\le n$, their sum $x+y$ would strictly exceed $q^n$.
Therefore, $x$ and $y$ must be linear combinations of strictly smaller elements (which correspond to higher powers): $q^{n+1}, q^{n+2}, \ldots$.
This implies $q^n \in \langle q^{n+1}, q^{n+2}, \ldots \rangle$, so we can write:
$$ q^n = \sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i q^{n+i} \quad (c_i \in \mathbb{N}_0) $$
Dividing both sides by $q^n$:
$$ 1 = \sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i q^i $$
Substituting $q = a/b$:
$$ 1 = \sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^i $$
Multiplying both sides by $b^m$:
$$ b^m = \sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i a^i b^{m-i} $$
Notice that every term on the right-hand side contains at least one factor of $a$ (since $i \ge 1$). Thus, $a$ must divide $b^m$.
Since $\gcd(a,b) = 1$, $a$ cannot divide $b^m$ unless $a = 1$. This directly contradicts our assumption that $a \ge 2$.
Hence, $q^n$ cannot be decomposed, making it an atom.

In both subcases, we proved that every power $q^n$ is an atom. Since every element in $M_q$ is, by definition, a sum of powers of $q$, every element factors into atoms. Thus, $M_q$ is atomic. Because any sum of two or more atoms cannot be an atom, the set of atoms is exactly the generating set $\{q^n : n \in \mathbb{N}_0\}$.
- **Is it atomic?** Yes.
- **Set of atoms:** $\{q^n : n \in \mathbb{N}_0\}$.

---

### Conclusion

The monoid $(\mathbb{N}_0[q], +)$ is atomic if and only if $q \in \mathbb{N}$ or $q = a/b$ with $a, b \ge 2$.
Equivalently, it is atomic for all $q \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ EXCEPT those of the form $q = 1/n$ for an integer $n \ge 2$.

- For $q \in \mathbb{N}$, the set of atoms is $\{1\}$.
- For $q = a/b$ with $a, b \ge 2$, the set of atoms is $\{q^n : n \in \mathbb{N}_0\}$.

## Notes

The proofs for $q > 1$ and $0 < q < 1$ are symmetric. In the $q > 1$ case, the obstruction to decomposing $q^n$ comes from the denominator $b$: the equation $a^n = \sum c_i a^i b^{n-i}$ forces $b \mid a^n$, impossible since $\gcd(a,b) = 1$ and $b \ge 2$. In the $0 < q < 1$ case, the obstruction comes from the numerator $a$: the analogous equation forces $a \mid b^m$, again impossible.

The non-atomic case $q = 1/b$ is also instructive: the monoid $\mathbb{N}_0[1/b]$ behaves like a localization at $b$, and the infinite divisibility $x = b \cdot (qx)$ prevents any atom from existing. This mirrors the fact that $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{\ge 0}$ has no atoms under addition.
