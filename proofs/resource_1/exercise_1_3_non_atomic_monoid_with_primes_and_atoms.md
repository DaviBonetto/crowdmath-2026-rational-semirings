## Restatement

Construct an explicit commutative monoid $M$ that is simultaneously:
1. **Non-atomic:** some element of $M$ has no factorization into atoms;
2. **Has infinitely many prime elements;**
3. **Has infinitely many atoms that are not prime.**

---

## Definitions Used

- **Atom:** A non-identity element $a \in M$ such that $a = xy$ implies $x = 1_M$ or $y = 1_M$.
- **Prime element:** A non-identity $p \in M$ such that $p \mid ab$ implies $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$.
- **Atomic monoid:** Every non-identity element factors as a finite product of atoms.
- **Direct product:** Given monoids $(A, +)$ and $(B, \cdot)$, the monoid $M = A \times B$ has operation $(a_1, b_1) \star (a_2, b_2) = (a_1 + a_2,\, b_1 \cdot b_2)$ and identity $(0_A, 1_B)$.

---

## Construction

Let:

$$N = \left\langle \frac{1}{2^n} : n \geq 1 \right\rangle \subseteq (\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}, +)$$

be the additive submonoid generated by $\{1/2, 1/4, 1/8, \ldots\}$, and let

$$H = \{4k + 1 : k \in \mathbb{N}_0\} = \{1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 21, \ldots\}$$

be the Hilbert monoid under multiplication. Define

$$\boxed{M = N \times H}$$

with the componentwise operation $(a_1, h_1) \star (a_2, h_2) = (a_1 + a_2,\, h_1 h_2)$ and identity $(0, 1)$.

---

## Proof

### Step 1: $M$ is a well-defined commutative monoid.

$N$ is an additive commutative monoid and $H$ is a multiplicative commutative monoid (since $(4a+1)(4b+1) = 4(4ab+a+b)+1 \in H$). Their direct product $M$ is therefore a commutative monoid. $\square$

---

### Step 2: $M$ is not atomic.

**Claim:** The element $(1, 1) \in M$ has no atomic factorization.

Suppose $(1, 1) = (a_1, h_1) \star (a_2, h_2)$ with both factors non-identity. Then:
- $h_1 h_2 = 1$ in $H \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, so $h_1 = h_2 = 1$;
- $a_1 + a_2 = 1$ with $a_i \in N$, $a_i \geq 0$, and $(a_i, 1) \neq (0, 1)$, so $a_i > 0$.

Thus any factorization of $(1, 1)$ in $M$ corresponds exactly to a factorization $1 = a_1 + a_2$ in $N$ with $a_1, a_2 > 0$.

**$1 \in N$ is not atomic:** Every generator $1/2^n \in N$ satisfies $1/2^n = 2 \cdot (1/2^{n+1})$, so no generator is an atom of $N$. In fact, $N$ has no atoms at all: any element $x \in N$ can be written as $x = 2 \cdot (x/2)$, and $x/2 \in N$ (since every element of $N$ has denominator a power of $2$). Hence every element of $N \setminus \{0\}$ is a non-trivial sum and $N$ has no atoms.

Moreover, any atom of $M$ with second coordinate $1$ would have to be of the form $(a, 1)$ where $a$ is an atom of $N$. But $N$ has no atoms. Therefore no factor appearing in a hypothetical atomic factorization of $(1, 1)$ can be an atom, because every factor must have second coordinate $1$.

Therefore $(1, 1) \in M$ has no atomic factorization, so $M$ is not atomic. $\blacksquare$

---

### Step 3: $M$ has infinitely many prime elements.

Let $\mathbb{P}_1 = \{p \in \mathbb{Z} : p \text{ prime},\, p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}\} = \{5, 13, 17, 29, 37, \ldots\}$.

By the infinitude of primes in each residue class coprime to $4$ (a consequence of Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions), $|\mathbb{P}_1| = \infty$.

By Exercise 1.2 Part (1), every $p \in \mathbb{P}_1$ is a prime element of $H$.

**Claim:** For each $p \in \mathbb{P}_1$, the element $(0, p) \in M$ is prime.

Suppose $(0, p) \mid_M (a_1, h_1) \star (a_2, h_2) = (a_1 + a_2, h_1 h_2)$. Then there exists $(e, f) \in M$ with
$$
(a_1 + a_2,\, h_1 h_2) = (0, p) \star (e, f) = (e,\, pf).
$$
So $h_1 h_2 = pf$, i.e., $p \mid_H h_1 h_2$. Since $p$ is prime in $H$, either $p \mid_H h_1$ or $p \mid_H h_2$.

- If $p \mid_H h_1$: write $h_1 = p \cdot g$ with $g \in H$. Then $(a_1, h_1) = (0, p) \star (a_1, g)$ with $(a_1, g) \in M$, so $(0, p) \mid_M (a_1, h_1)$.
- If $p \mid_H h_2$: similarly $(0, p) \mid_M (a_2, h_2)$.

So $(0, p)$ is a prime element of $M$. Since $|\mathbb{P}_1| = \infty$, $M$ has infinitely many prime elements. $\blacksquare$

---

### Step 4: $M$ has infinitely many atoms that are not prime.

Let $\mathbb{P}_3 = \{q \in \mathbb{Z} : q \text{ prime},\, q \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\} = \{3, 7, 11, 19, 23, \ldots\}$.

Since $|\mathbb{P}_3| = \infty$, we can form infinitely many products $c_n = q_{2n-1} q_{2n}$ with $q_{2n-1}, q_{2n} \in \mathbb{P}_3$ distinct. Each $c_n \in H$ (since $q_i \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ gives $c_n \equiv 1 \pmod 4$).

By Exercise 1.2 Part (2), each $c_n$ is an atom of $H$ that is not prime in $H$.

**Claim:** Each $(0, c_n) \in M$ is an atom of $M$ that is not prime.

**Atom:** Suppose $(0, c_n) = (a_1, h_1) \star (a_2, h_2)$ with both factors $\neq (0, 1)$. Then $a_1 + a_2 = 0$ in $N$ (so $a_1 = a_2 = 0$) and $h_1 h_2 = c_n$ with $h_1, h_2 \neq 1$ in $H$. But $c_n$ is an atom of $H$, so no such factorization exists. Hence $(0, c_n)$ is an atom of $M$. $\square$

**Not prime:** Since $c_n$ is not prime in $H$, there exist $x, y \in H$ with $c_n \mid_H xy$ but $c_n \nmid_H x$ and $c_n \nmid_H y$.

Then $(0, c_n) \mid_M (0, x) \star (0, y) = (0, xy)$ (with witness $(0, xy/c_n)$), but:
- $c_n \nmid_H x$ means $(0, c_n) \nmid_M (0, x)$ (any witness $(e, f)$ would require $x = c_n f$ in $H$);
- similarly $(0, c_n) \nmid_M (0, y)$.

So $(0, c_n)$ is not prime. $\square$

Since we obtain distinct atoms $(0, c_n)$ for each $n$ (as $c_n$ are distinct elements of $H$), $M$ has infinitely many non-prime atoms. $\blacksquare$

---

## Summary

The monoid $M = N \times H$, where $N = \langle 1/2^n : n \geq 1 \rangle$ and $H$ is the Hilbert monoid, satisfies all three requirements:

| Property | Witness | Verified by |
|---|---|---|
| Non-atomic | $(1, 1) \in M$ | $N$ has no atoms; any factorization of $(1,1)$ reduces to $N$ |
| Infinitely many primes | $\{(0, p) : p \in \mathbb{P}_1\}$ | Primes of $H$ embed as primes of $M$ |
| Infinitely many non-prime atoms | $\{(0, q_{2n-1}q_{2n}) : n \geq 1\}$ | Atoms-not-primes of $H$ embed as atoms-not-primes of $M$ |

---

## Notes

The direct product trick is the key: it lets us "stack" independently designed monoids. The non-atomic part ($N$) contributes a non-atomic element without interfering with $H$, because any factorization of $(1, 1)$ forces the $H$-component to stay at the identity.

What makes $N$ non-atomic is the infinite divisibility by $2$: every element of $N$ is divisible by $2$ again (just by halving), so the descent toward atoms never terminates. This is a typical Puiseux monoid phenomenon — the generators accumulate at $0$ and there is no smallest positive element to serve as an atom.

The Hilbert monoid provides both primes (from $\mathbb{P}_1$) and non-prime atoms (from products of pairs in $\mathbb{P}_3$). This is essentially the role of Exercise 1.2 in the construction.

*Note on Dirichlet's theorem:* The argument uses the standard fact that there are infinitely many primes congruent to $1$ and $3$ modulo $4$. Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions is the general result that justifies this.
